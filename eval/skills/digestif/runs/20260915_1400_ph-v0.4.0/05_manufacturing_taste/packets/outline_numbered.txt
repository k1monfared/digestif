L002:     Summary: A simulation finds quality only loosely filters canon success, exposure and capital dominate, so the canon and our taste are more contingent than they feel. (¶1, ¶35, ¶52)
L003:         - 1 The canon's winners may be the best, or merely the best positioned, and history cannot tell us which. (¶3, ¶4)
L004:             - 1.1 The question began half-jokingly: was Bach the Justin Bieber of his time? (¶2)
L005:             - 1.2 In 18th-century Vienna alone, somewhere between 200 and 500 composers were active at any given time. (¶3)
L006:             - 1.3 IMSLP catalogs over 3,400 Classical-era composers whose work survived, Wikipedia's list names several hundred, and many more left no trace; we remember fewer than 20. (¶3)
L007:             - 1.4 The standard explanation says we remember the best, but the winners may be those with the right patron, court position, orchestra, and publisher. (¶3)
L008:             - 1.5 If the Esterházys had hired a different Kapellmeister instead of Haydn, would we still know his name? (¶4)
L009:             - 1.6 That question cannot be answered empirically, but a model of cultural markets can be run thousands of times. (¶4)
L010:             - 1.7 The author built a simulation and wrote this less formal version of the full technical paper. (¶5)
L011:         - 2 Theme: Three competing hypotheses were set up to explain how canons form. (¶6)
L012:             - 2.1 Story 1 (The Cream Rises): the works we remember are the best works, and over long horizons genuine quality wins. (¶7)
L013:                 - 2.1.1 Money might buy a head start, but replaying history with different patrons would end with roughly the same canon. (¶7)
L014:             - 2.2 Story 2 (Money Writes History): success is mostly about who gets heard first, and quality is almost irrelevant. (¶8)
L015:                 - 2.2.1 A wealthy patron gets a composer performed, audiences like what they hear, and the familiarity gets read as quality. (¶8)
L016:                 - 2.2.2 Institutions lock that advantage in for future generations. (¶8)
L017:             - 2.3 Story 3 (Floors and Ceilings): both stories are partly right. (¶9)
L018:                 - 2.3.1 The truly terrible never succeed no matter how much money backs them. (¶9)
L019:                 - 2.3.2 The truly transcendent sometimes break through despite everything. (¶9)
L020:                 - 2.3.3 For the vast majority in the middle, fame depends on luck, money, and timing, not on talent differences with peers. (¶9)
L021:             - 2.4 Example: The canon is like a job market: the very best and the very worst candidates are easy to rank. (¶10)
L022:                 - 2.4.1 Example: Among twenty pretty good candidates, who gets hired often comes down to who knew someone, a good interview day, or resume order. (¶10)
L023:                 - 2.4.2 Nuance: The classical music canon is the same problem at civilizational scale. (¶10)
L024:         - 3 Theme: Three mechanisms, each individually well-documented, were combined in a way that had not been quantified. (¶11)
L025:             - 3.1 Mere exposure: we tend to like things we have encountered before. (¶12)
L026:                 - 3.1.1 Example: A song played on the radio ten times starts to feel catchy even if it did not grab you the first time. (¶12)
L027:                 - 3.1.2 This is not about learning to appreciate complexity; it happens with nonsense syllables, abstract shapes, and strangers' faces. (¶12)
L028:                 - 3.1.3 Evidence: Zajonc demonstrated the effect in 1968. (¶12) [reported]
L029:                 - 3.1.4 Evidence: Bornstein's 1989 meta-analysis across 208 experiments confirmed it with an average effect size of r = 0.26. (¶12) [reported]
L030:                 - 3.1.5 Nuance: The effect peaks at moderate exposure and reverses with overexposure. (¶12)
L031:                 - 3.1.6 Artists who get more initial airtime develop an artificial quality advantage, because audiences genuinely perceive funded work as better. (¶13)
L032:                 - 3.1.7 Nuance: The problem is not that people are bad judges; the judging is contaminated by exposure, and the exposure was determined by money. (¶13)
L033:             - 3.2 Social influence: we use what others consume as a shortcut for what is good. (¶14)
L034:                 - 3.2.1 Bestseller lists, download counts, trending labels, and view counts nudge us toward what is already popular, creating snowball effects. (¶14)
L035:                 - 3.2.2 Evidence: The 2006 Salganik MusicLab experiment let 14,341 participants choose among 48 unknown songs, some seeing download counts, others not. (¶14) [reported]
L036:                 - 3.2.3 Evidence: The same song ranked 1st in one version of the world and 40th in another, purely from random early differences in downloads. (¶14) [reported]
L037:             - 3.3 Cumulative advantage: the rich get richer, what the sociologist Robert Merton called the Matthew Effect. (¶15)
L038:                 - 3.3.1 A first album that sells well buys a bigger marketing budget, better tour slots, more press, and prominent playlist placement. (¶15)
L039:                 - 3.3.2 Nuance: Small initial differences, whether from luck, timing, or money, get magnified into enormous gaps over time. (¶15)
L040:                 - 3.3.3 Example: A composer who secures a court position at 20 accumulates decades of performances and reputation that a one-year miss never builds. (¶15)
L041:             - 3.4 Nuance: Each mechanism is well-understood alone; the open question is what happens when all three interact over many generations. (¶16)
L042:         - 4 Theme: The model was run thousands of times to test how social signals, money, and patronage shape the canon. (¶16, ¶19)
L043:             - 4.1 Model design: producers, consumers, and gatekeepers, with quality and capital drawn independently. (¶17)
L044:                 - 4.1.1 Each producer gets a random quality score and an independently drawn capital score, so being wealthy does not make you talented. (¶17)
L045:                 - 4.1.2 Producers with more capital get more exposure, and consumers form opinions from quality, mere exposure, and social signals. (¶18)
L046:                 - 4.1.3 Success feeds back into more exposure, and after many rounds some producers achieve canonical status. (¶18)
L047:                 - 4.1.4 Evidence: Each run took about two minutes with 1,000 producers and 10,000 consumers, parallelized across CPU cores. (¶19)
L048:                 - 4.1.5 Evidence: The author ran 360+ runs per condition for the main experiments, with formal power analysis to check sample sizes. (¶19)
L049:             - 4.2 Experiment 1: visible social influence weakens the quality-success link. (¶20)
L050:                 - 4.2.1 Evidence: When listeners could see others' consumption, the quality-success correlation dropped significantly (p < 0.0001 across 360 runs per condition). (¶20)
L051:                 - 4.2.2 In a world where nobody could see what anyone else was listening to, quality would be a moderately good predictor of success. (¶20)
L052:                 - 4.2.3 In our world of charts, recommendations, and trending labels, the link gets weaker. (¶20)
L053:                     - 4.2.3.1 A mediocre work that gets lucky early can ride social proof to stardom. (¶20)
L054:                     - 4.2.3.2 An equally good work that starts slow may never recover. (¶20)
L055:                 - 4.2.4 Every Top 40 chart, Customers Also Bought widget, and view count is a form of social influence pushing us away from independent judgment. (¶21)
L056:                 - 4.2.5 The measurement trap: popularity is used as a proxy for quality, then optimized for, then forgotten as a proxy. (¶21)
L057:             - 4.3 Experiment 2: holding talent constant and reshuffling only resources produces almost entirely different canons. (¶22)
L058:                 - 4.3.1 Evidence: The counterfactual distance was 0.88 on a 0-to-1 scale, where 0 means identical canons and 1 means completely different ones. (¶23)
L059:                 - 4.3.2 Only the top 10% of talent had a meaningful shot at fame regardless of funding, and even then only about one in four. (¶24)
L060:                 - 4.3.3 For everyone in the 50th to 90th percentile of quality, canonical status was effectively a lottery. (¶24)
L061:                 - 4.3.4 This is the simulation equivalent of the Haydn question: excellence alone is not enough when the exposure pipeline is narrow. (¶25)
L062:                 - 4.3.5 Had the Esterházys hired someone else, Haydn would very likely be one of the hundreds of forgotten composers. (¶25)
L063:             - 4.4 Experiment 3: unequal money distorts the canon more than herd behavior. (¶26, ¶28)
L064:                 - 4.4.1 Evidence: Quality-canon correlation by condition: full model 0.32, no social influence 0.35, equal capital 0.46, quality only 0.46. (¶27)
L065:                 - 4.4.2 Turning off social influence barely helps, but equalizing starting resources jumps the quality-canon correlation by 44%. (¶28)
L066:                 - 4.4.3 The biggest distortion is not that audiences follow trends; it is that some artists never get heard in the first place. (¶28)
L067:                 - 4.4.4 Capital gates access to the exposure pipeline, and everything downstream of that is noise amplification. (¶29)
L068:                 - 4.4.5 The modern mapping: the problem is not that people blindly follow Spotify playlists, but that only major-label artists get on them. (¶29)
L069:             - 4.5 Experiment 4: a stylized Vienna run used 300 composers, a handful of very wealthy patrons, strong word-of-mouth, and no recording technology. (¶30)
L070:                 - 4.5.1 Evidence: Its counterfactual distance was 0.97, near maximum, with only 2.5% of canonical composers shared between any two alternate histories. (¶30)
L071:                 - 4.5.2 Rewinding to 1750 and randomly reassigning court positions would make the names we teach almost entirely different. (¶31)
L072:                 - 4.5.3 The winners were very likely excellent, since you had to be good to compete for a court position. (¶31)
L073:                 - 4.5.4 Dozens of equally excellent composers were lost to history because they drew the short straw in the patronage lottery. (¶31)
L074:                 - 4.5.5 Manufacturing taste means the winners were not bad, but the losers were not meaningfully worse. (¶32)
L075:             - 4.6 Robustness: the results held with every parameter varied by ±50%. (¶33)
L076:                 - 4.6.1 The single most important factor is how steeply money converts into attention. (¶33)
L077:                 - 4.6.2 If doubling a marketing budget more than doubles the audience, the rich get richer very fast and quality barely matters. (¶33)
L078:                 - 4.6.3 If doubling a budget less than doubles the audience, quality has more room to breathe. (¶33)
L079:                 - 4.6.4 Evidence: In the industry, a major label can buy a front-page Spotify placement reaching 50 million listeners while an independent artist's self-promotion reaches 500. (¶34)
L080:                 - 4.6.5 Under such an extremely steep capital-to-exposure curve, quality has very little to do with who becomes famous. (¶34)
L081:         - 5 Story 3 wins: quality is a loose filter that sets a floor and a ceiling, and the vast middle is dominated by capital and luck. (¶35, ¶36)
L082:             - 5.1 Nuance: The greatest composer who ever lived, made precise, is the greatest among those with the resources to be heard, published, and preserved. (¶37)
L083:                 - 5.1.1 Nuance: There may have been equally gifted composers whose music was never performed, never published, and never survived. (¶37)
L084:                 - 5.1.2 Nuance: We cannot know, but the simulation says it is very likely. (¶37)
L085:             - 5.2 The mechanisms are stronger today, not weaker. (¶38)
L086:                 - 5.2.1 Example: An 18th-century patronage decision played out over decades; a Spotify playlist placement plays out in hours. (¶38)
L087:                 - 5.2.2 The scale of winner-take-all dynamics is vastly larger than anything in the historical record. (¶38)
L088:             - 5.3 The highest-leverage intervention is not better judgment, it is wider exposure. (¶39)
L089:                 - 5.3.1 Audiences are already decent judges within the limits of what they are exposed to; the bottleneck is exposure itself. (¶39)
L090:                 - 5.3.2 Evidence: Blind auditions for orchestras, widely adopted in the 1970s-80s, raised the proportion of women hired by major US orchestras from 5% to 25%. (¶39)
L091:                 - 5.3.3 The talent was always there; the barrier was exposure. (¶39)
L092:             - 5.4 The deeper pattern is the gap between the metric and what we care about: canon survival is a proxy for being the best. (¶40)
L093:                 - 5.4.1 The simulation does not say what the canon should look like, only that the one we got is far more contingent than we assume. (¶40)
L094:         - 6 Theme: Three caveats bound what the model can tell us. (¶41)
L095:             - 6.1 Nuance: The model assumes quality is a real, stable property independent of who hears it, which is philosophically debatable. (¶42)
L096:                 - 6.1.1 Nuance: If quality is partly constituted by social processes, the whole decomposition may be ill-posed. (¶42)
L097:             - 6.2 Nuance: The calibration data comes from American teenagers evaluating pop songs over weeks; generalizing to 18th-century European aristocrats over decades requires a leap of faith. (¶43)
L098:                 - 6.2.1 Nuance: The mechanisms are well-documented across many contexts, but the specific parameter values are uncertain. (¶43)
L099:             - 6.3 Nuance: The model omits genre formation, critical discourse, technological change, and cultural politics. (¶44)
L100:                 - 6.3.1 Nuance: Any model is a simplification; the direction of the effects is robust across parameter values, even if the magnitudes are uncertain. (¶44)
L101:         - 7 Bach was excellent AND well-positioned, and the second half of that conjunction matters more than the first. (¶45, ¶47)
L102:             - 7.1 The Bieber analogy gets the mechanism right but the quality floor wrong. (¶46)
L103:                 - 7.1.1 The pop pipeline can manufacture a global phenomenon from someone good enough to carry a tune and look good on camera, a bar millions meet. (¶46)
L104:                 - 7.1.2 A court position in Bach's world required writing for liturgical services, entertaining aristocrats, training choirs, and improvising fugues on demand. (¶46)
L105:                 - 7.1.3 Nuance: The quality floor of that world was genuinely high. (¶46)
L106:             - 7.2 Reshuffling patronage gives an almost entirely different canon, with only 2.5% overlap between alternate histories. (¶47)
L107:             - 7.3 Example: Bach is like the one surgeon who gets famous out of fifty equally skilled surgeons at major hospitals. (¶48)
L108:                 - 7.3.1 Example: A New York Times profile led to speaking invitations, a book deal, and a reputation as the best surgeon in the country. (¶48)
L109:                 - 7.3.2 Nuance: The other 49 are just as good, and you have never heard of them. (¶48)
L110:             - 7.4 The question was never whether Bach was great; he almost certainly was. (¶49)
L111:                 - 7.4.1 The question is whether we listen because he was the greatest, or because he was great AND landed the right church job in Leipzig. (¶49)
L112:                 - 7.4.2 The simulation says it is the second. (¶49)
L113:                 - 7.4.3 Nuance: There were probably 20 to 50 of his contemporaries who were equally great, whose names we will never know. (¶49)
L114:             - 7.5 The canon is one canon out of hundreds of equally valid ones we could have ended up with, and we treat it as inevitable. (¶50)
L115:             - 7.6 Bach's harmonic language, his structures, and his aesthetic sensibility are the foundations of how we hear and judge music today. (¶51)
L116:                 - 7.6.1 With a different set of composers surviving into the canon, we would have inherited different foundations and would hear and judge differently. (¶51)
L117:                 - 7.6.2 What we call good taste would be a different taste entirely. (¶51)
L118:             - 7.7 Our taste was manufactured along with the canon: we like what we were taught to like. (¶52)
L119:                 - 7.7.1 The survivors were selected by money and luck as much as by merit. (¶52)
L120:                 - 7.7.2 Nuance: The whole chain from who got funded to what counts as beautiful is more contingent than it feels from the inside. (¶52)
L121:             - 7.8 Whether people 300 years from now will listen to Bieber and call it the peak of our era is unknown. (¶53)
L122:                 - 7.8.1 If the mechanisms are right, the 2000s canon will track distribution deals and the most favorable algorithms more than the best music. (¶53)
L123:                 - 7.8.2 Nuance: And we will not be around to correct them. (¶53)
