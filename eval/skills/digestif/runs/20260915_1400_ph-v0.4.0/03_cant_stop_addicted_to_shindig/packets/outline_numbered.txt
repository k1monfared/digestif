L002:     Summary: Can't Stop's four-dice probabilities and forced-move rule make it nearly balanced, and simulations show probabilistic, consistent play wins, though going first is a large advantage. (¶1, ¶2, ¶95, ¶96, ¶98, ¶100)
L003:         - 1 Can't Stop has players roll four dice, pair them into sums, and advance markers on columns 2 through 12, needing three completed columns to win. (¶3, ¶4, ¶5)
L004:             - 1.1 You can keep rolling as long as you want, but busting when no marker can move loses the turn's gains. (¶3)
L005:             - 1.2 Column lengths run 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 11, 9, 7, 5, and 3 steps for columns 2 through 12. (¶4)
L006:             - 1.3 The forced-move rule requires taking all legal moves from a chosen pairing, even unwanted ones. (¶6)
L007:             - 1.4 Example: On {6,7,8}, rolling (1,1,3,4) and pairing 2+7 forces moving both 2 and 7 as well. (¶6)
L008:             - 1.5 Nuance: The forced-move rule prevents trivial play and adds tactical depth. (¶6)
L009:             - 1.6 Evidence: An interactive web version exists with a Python/FastAPI backend and React frontend. (¶5)
L010:         - 2 Four dice flatten the distribution: P(7) rises to 64.4% and P(2) to 13.2%, far closer together than with two dice. (¶7, ¶15, ¶21)
L011:             - 2.1 With two dice, sum 7 is most likely at 16.67% and exactly 6× more likely than sum 2 at 2.78%. (¶8, ¶9)
L012:                 - 2.1.1 Evidence: Sum 2 requires both dice showing 1 (1/36); sum 7 has 6 order-sensitive ways: (1,6), (2,5), (3,4). (¶9)
L013:             - 2.2 Nuance: If Can't Stop used two dice, column 7 would need to be 6× longer than column 2, but it is only 13/3 ≈ 4.3×. (¶10)
L014:             - 2.3 Example: Rolling 2, 3, 4, 5 gives just three pairings: 5 and 9, 6 and 8, or 7 and 7. (¶11)
L015:             - 2.4 Four dice give P(can make a 2) = 171/1296 ≈ 13.2%, requiring at least two 1s among the four. (¶12, ¶13, ¶14)
L016:                 - 2.4.1 Evidence: P(zero 1s) = 625/1296 and P(exactly one 1) = 500/1296, so P(can make 2) = 1 - 625/1296 - 500/1296. (¶13, ¶14)
L017:             - 2.5 Four dice give P(can make a 7) = 834/1296 ≈ 64.4%, via inclusion-exclusion over pairs (1,6), (2,5), (3,4). (¶15, ¶16, ¶20)
L018:                 - 2.5.1 Evidence: Each pair event has probability 302/1296, each pairwise overlap 24/1296, and the triple overlap is 0. (¶17, ¶18, ¶19)
L019:                 - 2.5.2 Evidence: Final sum: 302 + 302 + 302 - 24 - 24 - 24 + 0 = 834/1296 ≈ 64.4%. (¶20)
L020:             - 2.6 Evidence: The full four-dice table shows at-least-one-pair counts from 171 (13.2%) for sum 2 up to 834 (64.4%) for sum 7. (¶22, ¶23)
L021:             - 2.7 The probability ratio from column 2 to 7 is 4.88 against a length ratio of 4.33, close enough to suggest deliberate design. (¶24)
L022:             - 2.8 The conclusion restates this: four-dice probabilities flatten the distribution, with P(7) = 64.4% versus P(2) = 13.2%, only a 4.88× difference. (¶96)
