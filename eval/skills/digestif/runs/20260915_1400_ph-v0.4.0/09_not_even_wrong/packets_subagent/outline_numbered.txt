L001: - Not Even Wrong: what we believe and why, point graph (source: 09_not_even_wrong/source.txt)
L002:     Summary: Unverifiable not-even-wrong claims persuade through investment, identity, and social proof rather than evidence and reasoning. (¶1, ¶2, ¶18)
L003:         - 1 Theme: Not-even-wrong ideas are so fundamentally flawed or ill-constructed they cannot be meaningfully evaluated as either correct or incorrect. (¶2)
L004:             - 1.1 Theoretical physicist Wolfgang Pauli popularized the phrase 'not even wrong'. (¶2)
L005:             - 1.2 Example: At 89, having earned both the Fields Medal and Abel Prize, Atiyah claimed to solve the Riemann hypothesis, open since 1859. (¶3)
L006:             - 1.3 Many called the proof 'not even wrong': lacking necessary rigor and coherence, it wasn't just incorrect but outside traditional evaluation. (¶4)
L007:             - 1.4 Many powerful figures' rhetoric sounds compelling but functions outside traditional evaluation, extending the concept beyond math. (¶5)
L008:         - 2 Theme: Financial, social, or ethical investment in visionaries triggers belief-reinforcing psychology. (¶6)
L009:             - 2.1 The financial stake creates a powerful incentive to believe in the leader's vision and competence. (¶6)
L010:             - 2.2 It isn't just about mentally protecting the investment; it's about resolving cognitive dissonance. (¶6)
L011:             - 2.3 Committed investors become more receptive to claims even outside the leader's areas of demonstrated expertise. (¶7)
L012:             - 2.4 Each successful product or milestone reinforces trust, easing acceptance of bold claims about future innovations without rigorous scrutiny. (¶7)
L013:             - 2.5 Self-reinforcing cycle: financial investment to emotional investment to receptivity, which can lead to further financial investment. (¶8)
L014:             - 2.6 The cycle combines material self-interest with identity formation; being a supporter becomes part of who someone is. (¶8)
L015:             - 2.7 A founder might pair verifiable promises about current products with grandiose, unfalsifiable claims about transforming civilization. (¶9)
L016:             - 2.8 Fulfilled concrete promises lend credibility to the grandiose ones, though they rest on completely different evidentiary standards. (¶9)
L017:         - 3 Theme: Popularity and outsider framing create tribal social proof, amplified by platforms. (¶10)
L018:             - 3.1 Thousands or millions praising a leader's genius provides social proof that their visions are credible. (¶10)
L019:             - 3.2 Humans naturally look to others for cues about what to believe. (¶10)
L020:             - 3.3 Influential figures often position themselves as outsiders; questioning claims can feel like betraying the tribe or siding with the opposition. (¶11)
L021:             - 3.4 Algorithms tend to reinforce existing beliefs via echo chambers where supporters mainly see positive content. (¶12)
L022:             - 3.5 Frequent, seemingly unfiltered posts create a sense of personal connection and authenticity that strengthens emotional investment. (¶12)
L023:         - 4 Theme: We are not purely rational beings who evaluate each claim independently on evidence. (¶13)
L024:             - 4.1 Evaluations are deeply influenced by financial interests, desire for consistency, need for belonging, and attraction to narratives. (¶13)
L025:             - 4.2 Factors aligned behind a leader or company can override critical faculties, leading us to accept claims we might otherwise scrutinize. (¶14)
L026:             - 4.3 Atiyah's claim was rejected by peers; business and political leaders' statements often escape rigorous evaluation; society lacks comparable frameworks. (¶15)
L027:         - 5 Theme: Paths forward: personal practices for individuals and broader societal measures. (¶16, ¶17)
L028:             - 5.1 Individuals: separate finance from belief; investing can be sound without accepting all leadership claims. (¶16)
L029:             - 5.2 Individuals: practice domain-specific evaluation; expertise doesn't transfer automatically across fields. (¶16)
L030:             - 5.3 Individuals: expose yourself to thoughtful criticism from credible sources in relevant domains, not critics for critics' sake. (¶16)
L031:             - 5.4 Individuals: adopt investment detachment; ask whether you'd believe the claim without the investment. (¶16)
L032:             - 5.5 Society: teach technological literacy, elevate domain experts, build assessment frameworks, normalize skepticism without animosity. (¶17)
L033:         - 6 Theme: Beyond tribal thinking: the closing case for critical tools and shared standards. (¶18)
L034:             - 6.1 Not for or against any figure: the challenge is developing tools to engage critically while appreciating innovations they may offer. (¶18)
L035:             - 6.2 Recognize that financial and emotional investment can cloud judgment and consciously separate claim evaluation from support or opposition. (¶19)
L036:             - 6.3 Evaluating claims independently of investments and affiliations may be one of the most important skills, navigating between blind acceptance and reflexive rejection. (¶20)
L037:             - 6.4 Healthy societies, like scientific fields, need shared standards; balancing openness with evidence is perhaps the central challenge. (¶21)
L038:             - 6.5 We might sympathize with Atiyah's attempt while valuing the community's commitment to rigorous standards, our best hope across domains. (¶22)
L039:             - 6.6 Evidence: Calling Bullshit addresses statements directly, narrow in focus but broadly applicable; Surveillance Capitalism covers narrative-steering and belief formation indirectly. (¶23)
