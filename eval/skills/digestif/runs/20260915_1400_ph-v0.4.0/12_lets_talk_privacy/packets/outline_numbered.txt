L002:     Summary: E2EE trust varies by architecture: WhatsApp mediates keys, Proton stores password-encrypted keys, Signal never holds them, and plaintext password storage stays unverifiable. (¶1, ¶3, ¶43)
L003:         - 1 End-to-end encryption can mean several things in practice, and the real question is whether the provider can access your data. (¶3)
L004:             - 1.1 The author is not a security expert and writes this as an attempt to understand privacy as an end user. (¶2)
L005:             - 1.2 Encrypting on the sender's device and decrypting on the receiver's device without decryption along the way is the definition of E2EE. (¶3)
L006:             - 1.3 If the purpose is only to defeat a man-in-the-middle attack, most implementations should be good enough. (¶3)
L007:             - 1.4 Usually the purpose is that no one, and in particular the provider, can access the data. (¶3)
L008:             - 1.5 Example: With WhatsApp, you do not want Facebook to access message content, though it still knows your network, communication frequency, amount, and times. (¶3)
L009:             - 1.6 The real question is whether Facebook can access the content of your WhatsApp messages in theory and in practice. (¶3)
L010:         - 2 WhatsApp's answer is not completely clear: it depends on unverifiable implementation claims. (¶4)
L011:             - 2.1 If they implemented things as claimed and added no secret backdoors, then yes, they cannot access the data. (¶4)
L012:             - 2.2 We will not really know, because the app is not open source and hence not really auditable. (¶4)
L013:             - 2.3 External audits and tests lack certainty in the negative direction: they can never claim it cannot access the data. (¶4)
L014:             - 2.4 The 2021 privacy policy changes allow sharing data with Facebook for relevant offers and ads across Meta company products. (¶4)
L015:             - 2.5 Previous security failures include storing hundreds of millions of passwords in plaintext. (¶4)
L016:             - 2.6 Given the company's business, you can almost be certain that they can, and do, access the messages. (¶4)
L017:             - 2.7 A smoking gun is whether the platform or app can back up your data. (¶5)
L018:                 - 2.7.1 It is whether you can access history across devices without manually linking them somehow. (¶5)
L019:             - 2.8 Encryption is locking something with a key: one that only locks and that everyone has, and a master key only the receiver holds. (¶5)
L020:             - 2.9 Your master key is created and stored on your phone by default when you create an account. (¶5)
L021:             - 2.10 In a simple implementation, another device should not have the same master key to decrypt past messages. (¶5)
L022:                 - 2.10.1 This is what happens in Signal, where you somehow copy the key from your original device to the new one. (¶5)
L023:             - 2.11 If at any time Facebook has access to the key, the situation is compromised. (¶5)
L024:                 - 2.11.1 Example: It is as if you locked your door and left a copy at the local farmers market billboard. (¶5)
L025:             - 2.12 WhatsApp's official documentation says the server stores encrypted message history and maintains mappings between your account and your device identities. (¶6)
L026:             - 2.13 When you add a new device, the server authenticates it and provides the keys needed to decrypt your message history. (¶6)
L027:             - 2.14 This is fundamentally different from Signal: the server is a trusted intermediary that controls access to your encryption keys. (¶6)
L028:             - 2.15 Meta could theoretically authenticate a device that is not yours and give it access to decrypt your messages. (¶6)
L029:                 - 2.15.1 The claimed safeguards, device lists you can check and security codes you can verify, depend on the server accurately reporting what devices exist. (¶6)
L030:                 - 2.15.2 Since they control that server, they could simply hide a rogue device from your device list entirely. (¶6)
L031:             - 2.16 Evidence: The multi-device description comes from Meta's engineering post on how WhatsApp enables multi-device capability, from July 2021. (¶44) [reported]
L032:         - 3 Proton stores your encrypted private key on its servers, so the trust shifts to the client. (¶7, ¶14)
L033:             - 3.1 When you create a Proton account, your private key is generated in your browser and encrypted with your password. (¶7)
L034:                 - 3.1.1 The encryption uses bcrypt and AES-256 before the key is sent to Proton's servers for storage. (¶7)
L035:             - 3.2 The crucial point: your encrypted private key lives on Proton's servers, not just on your original device. (¶7)
L036:             - 3.3 On a new device or browser, the login sequence runs as follows. (¶8)
L037:                 - 3.3.1 Evidence: You type your password, and your browser hashes it with bcrypt using a salt provided by Proton's server. (¶9)
L038:                 - 3.3.2 Evidence: The hashed password is sent to Proton to prove you know the password. (¶9)
L039:                 - 3.3.3 Evidence: The server sends your encrypted private key back to your browser. (¶9)
L040:                 - 3.3.4 Evidence: Your browser uses your original password to decrypt the private key locally, and now you can read your emails. (¶9)
L041:             - 3.4 Hashing is one-way: you can turn a password into a hash, but you theoretically cannot reverse a hash back into the password. (¶10, ¶11)
L042:             - 3.5 Proton receives your hashed password to verify you are who you claim to be, but this hash alone should not reveal your password. (¶11)
L043:             - 3.6 Encryption is two-way: you encrypt data with a key and decrypt it with that same key or a paired one. (¶12)
L044:             - 3.7 Your private key is encrypted with your actual password, not the hash, so it can be decrypted when you provide the password again. (¶12)
L045:             - 3.8 The security model: Proton receives your hashed password, but the private key is encrypted with your actual password, so the hash cannot decrypt it. (¶13)
L046:             - 3.9 There is a critical vulnerability: Proton could serve malicious client code, in the web app or through an app update. (¶14)
L047:                 - 3.9.1 That code could capture your actual password when you type it, before it gets hashed. (¶14)
L048:             - 3.10 Since they already store your encrypted private key on their servers, if they capture your password they can decrypt it. (¶14)
L049:             - 3.11 This is why having open-source clients matters: the community can verify that the client is not doing anything malicious. (¶15)
L050:             - 3.12 But you still have to trust several things. (¶15)
L051:                 - 3.12.1 That the code they are actually serving matches the open-source code. (¶15)
L052:                 - 3.12.2 That they will not be compelled to inject malicious code in the future. (¶15)
L053:                 - 3.12.3 That their infrastructure has not been compromised by someone else who could serve malicious code. (¶15)
L054:             - 3.13 The crucial difference from WhatsApp is that Proton does not have direct access to your keys in a usable form. (¶16)
L055:             - 3.14 They would need to actively compromise the client to steal your password first, while WhatsApp's server can directly grant key access. (¶16)
L056:             - 3.15 Evidence: Proton documents key storage and zero-access encryption in its support and security pages. (¶45) [reported]
L057:         - 4 Signal never holds your keys: its servers are never in a position to grant access to your message history. (¶17, ¶19)
L058:             - 4.1 By default, message history is stored only on your device and deleted from Signal's servers after delivery. (¶17)
L059:             - 4.2 Switching devices leaves you three options. (¶17)
L060:             - 4.3 Manual local backup on Android: create an encrypted backup file protected by a 30-digit passphrase, then physically transfer it to your new device. (¶18)
L061:             - 4.4 Direct device-to-device transfer: both devices must be physically present and you transfer everything locally over your network. (¶18)
L062:             - 4.5 Signal Secure Backups: messages are encrypted with a 64-character recovery key that Signal never sees or has access to. (¶18)
L063:             - 4.6 Even with the newer cloud backup feature, the recovery key stays on your device. (¶19)
L064:                 - 4.6.1 Signal literally cannot decrypt your backups even if they wanted to, and cannot help you recover them if you lose the key. (¶19)
L065:             - 4.7 This is genuinely zero-knowledge architecture, not just marketing claims. (¶19)
L066:             - 4.8 Evidence: Signal documents Secure Backups in a September 2024 blog post and support article. (¶46) [reported]
L067:         - 5 Even trusting the encryption architecture, plaintext password storage on servers cannot be ruled out. (¶20, ¶21)
L068:             - 5.1 If they have your password and your encrypted private key, they can decrypt everything. (¶20)
L069:             - 5.2 The short answer is that we do not know for certain. (¶21)
L070:             - 5.3 You can verify what the client does if it is open source: that passwords are hashed before transmission. (¶21)
L071:             - 5.4 But you cannot verify what happens on the server after your password or password hash arrives; the server code is almost always closed source. (¶21)
L072:             - 5.5 Even with security audits, you are trusting the server operator. (¶21)
L073:                 - 5.5.1 That the audited code is what is actually running. (¶22)
L074:                 - 5.5.2 That they have not changed it since the audit. (¶22)
L075:                 - 5.5.3 That no rogue employee has modified it. (¶22)
L076:                 - 5.5.4 That their infrastructure has not been breached. (¶22)
L077:             - 5.6 Real-world examples show this is not just paranoia. (¶23)
L078:                 - 5.6.1 Evidence: Facebook disclosed in 2019 that they had been accidentally logging hundreds of millions of passwords in plaintext in internal logs for years. (¶23) [reported]
L079:                 - 5.6.2 Evidence: Adobe's 2013 breach revealed they stored password hints in plaintext. (¶23) [reported]
L080:                 - 5.6.3 Evidence: Many smaller services store passwords in plaintext or use weak hashing like MD5 without salting. (¶23) [reported]
L081:             - 5.7 For Proton specifically: resetting your password via email or SMS recovery loses access to all your old encrypted emails. (¶24)
L082:                 - 5.7.1 That suggests they genuinely cannot decrypt your data without your password. (¶24)
L083:             - 5.8 But even this is not proof: they could deliberately withhold access to maintain their privacy-focused image, even if they technically could decrypt. (¶24)
L084:         - 6 The fundamental problem: you are trusting reputation, jurisdiction, business model, and past behavior rather than cryptographically verifiable guarantees. (¶25)
L085:             - 6.1 Architecture matters: with WhatsApp, you are trusting Meta's entire server infrastructure. (¶26)
L086:             - 6.2 With Proton, you are trusting that they will not serve malicious code. (¶26)
L087:             - 6.3 With Signal and recovery keys, the attack surface is much smaller: they would need to compromise your device or steal your recovery key. (¶26)
L088:         - 7 Theme: The comparison tables contrast key generation, key storage, new-device behavior, provider device injection, server storage, required trust, and security model. (¶27)
L089:             - 7.1 Can the provider add a device without your knowledge? WhatsApp yes; Proton no, but could serve malicious client code; Signal no. (¶27)
L090:             - 7.2 What the server stores: WhatsApp keeps encrypted history, keys, and device-to-key mappings; Proton keeps emails and the encrypted private key; Signal keeps nothing by default. (¶27)
L091:             - 7.3 Security model names: server-mediated key escrow for WhatsApp, password-encrypted server-side key storage for Proton, client-controlled keys for Signal. (¶27, ¶42)
L092:         - 8 Theme: The verification question gets its own treatment: what can and cannot be checked. (¶28)
L093:             - 8.1 What you can verify: open-source clients hash or encrypt passwords before sending, though this proves only what the client does. (¶29)
L094:             - 8.2 Network traffic inspection with tools like Wireshark shows what is transmitted; plaintext over the network is a red flag. (¶30)
L095:                 - 8.2.1 Even with HTTPS encryption, you cannot see what the server does after decryption. (¶30)
L096:             - 8.3 Security audits: independent auditors can review server code if given access, but you trust the auditor and that the audited code was deployed. (¶31)
L097:             - 8.4 What you cannot verify: server-side code is almost always closed source, and even if shown to auditors you cannot verify it is what runs. (¶32)
L098:             - 8.5 Even if truthful during an audit, they could add password logging in a later update. (¶33)
L099:             - 8.6 An employee with server access could modify the code, or someone who breaches their systems could inject logging. (¶34)
L100:             - 8.7 Good signs: open-source server code, independent audits with published results, and breach disclosure where passwords were not compromised. (¶35)
L101:             - 8.8 Bad signs: being able to reset a password and access old data, security through obscurity, and a history of privacy violations. (¶36)
L102:             - 8.9 Evidence: Facebook 2019: between 200-600 million passwords were logged in plaintext and searchable by over 20,000 Facebook employees. (¶38) [reported]
L103:                 - 8.9.1 Evidence: Some archives dated back to 2012, and Facebook claimed the logging was unintentional. (¶38) [reported]
L104:             - 8.10 Evidence: Adobe 2013: the breach revealed stored password hints in plaintext, making brute-forcing easier, though at least passwords were hashed. (¶37) [reported]
L105:             - 8.11 Evidence: Many small services store passwords in plaintext or use MD5 without salt, and you find out when they get breached. (¶39) [reported]
L106:             - 8.12 You are always trusting the server operator to some degree unless one of three conditions holds. (¶40)
L107:                 - 8.12.1 All cryptography happens client-side, like Signal with recovery keys. (¶41)
L108:                 - 8.12.2 The server never sees anything that could decrypt your data, as with zero-knowledge proof systems. (¶41)
L109:                 - 8.12.3 Server code is open-source and reproducibly built, and you can verify what is running. (¶41)
L110:         - 9 The bottom line: for most commercial services you trust reputation, jurisdiction, business model, and past behavior rather than cryptographic guarantees. (¶43)
L111:             - 9.1 Security-conscious people prefer client-side encryption, open-source everything, decentralized systems that minimize trust, and hardware keys like YubiKey that never expose the secret. (¶43)
